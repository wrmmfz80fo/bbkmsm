When in a jam, what should take priority? Scientists looked at this very question in a recent study in the journal Communications Medicine. Their unanimous suggestion? Choose sleep. Here's why.

Sleep vs. Exercise: What Matters More for Health?

Researchers collected data for about 3.5 years from nearly 71,000 users of two health devices. One was a sleep sensor that goes under your mattress and the other a health tracker worn on the wrist.They found that people who got a good night’s rest (the recommended seven to nine hours) were more likely to be physically active the next day. This suggested that more sleep didn't kick activity out of their days entirely. Instead, sleep may open the door for more exercise.

In contrast, those who didn’t get enough sleep (specifically taking more time to go to bed and not getting enough sleep overall) tended to take fewer steps the following day. A lot fewer: While full-night sleepers averaged around 8,000 steps, the low sleepers only came in at about 5,000 a day.

“It really is challenging to maintain good sleep and physical activity whilst juggling the demands of daily life,” says Josh Fitton, lead study author and a research associate in sleep health at Flinders University.

Still if you had to choose one over the other, it's better to hit the hay. Scientists observed a person’s step count didn’t impact much of their sleep the next day. But when people got enough sleep, they were more likely to get in more activity the next day.

“Our findings support the notion that high-quality sleep within the recommended range—seven to nine hours a night—increases your capacity for physical activity,” says Fitton.

The caveat: The study only looked at step count as a measure for physical activity. So, the relationship between sleep and exercise isn't clear if people did or didn't do other workouts during the study. There could have been people who were physically active in other ways like weight lifting and Pilates and just didn't get enough steps in for the day. Still, the results suggest good sleep opens the door for more exercise.

Why Doctors Want You to Choose Sleep Over Exercise

When MH spoke to doctors who weren't involved in this study about its findings, they agreed it makes sense to prioritize sleep for a couple of reasons.

Both sleep and exercise can affect the immune system, mood, metabolism, and weight management. However, Beth Malow, MD, director of the Vanderbilt Sleep Division, says sleep is more impactful in this role.

“If you’re not getting enough sleep, you’re trying to bail out a boat with a hole in it,” says W. Christopher Winter, MD, a neurologist and sleep medicine physician with Charlottesville Neurology and Sleep Medicine and host of the Sleep Unplugged podcast. “You’re not really making any meaningful health gains.” So if you only slept for 5.5 hours and are faced with the work out or sleep dilemma, Winter recommends going back to bed because "the lack of sleep will hurt you faster.”

“When we feel well-rested, motivated, and energized after a good night’s sleep, our ability to engage in activity appears to be enhanced,” Fitton adds.

Exercise Still Matters—Just After Sleep

So even if it made sense to hit the snooze instead of get up and work out, see if there are other opportunities to get activity in that day. It doesn't have to be a full-hour gym session. Even a 15-minute workout workout is still exercise, Winter points out. Micro-workouts have benefits, too, as does simply taking the stairs or fitting in some core work between calls.

“We need to think about exercise like brushing our teeth,” he says. “If you have time to brush your teeth, you probably have time for exercise.”

Choosing sleep over a workout can be the right choice sometimes. But if you're always hitting snooze and never hitting the gym, you might need to rethink your schedule so you can get the benefits of both.
