# Turns Out, Being a Superfan Has Major Mental Health Benefits

[Turns Out](https://pipeprospect.com/category/p4z06i7)

I REMEMBER WHEN I first heard the Dead. It was about a week into COVID lockdowns. I had listened to the Grateful Dead at length before the pandemic, but there was something about “Sugaree” live from Veneta, Oregon, on August 27, 1972—blasted into my ears sometime in March of 2020 in the Mojave Desert outside Las Vegas—that sent me on a new adventure that wasn’t just sonic. That listen led me to a group of people I’ve come to love, stand among at live shows, converse with online, and, ultimately, belong to: Deadheads.

[Being Superfan](https://peplux.cc/x7vamucjhx/)

Community. Belonging. It’s a necessary part of us that we can’t get on our own. It takes stepping outside ourselves and interacting with others, agreeing on and uniting around something larger than us all. Die-hard sports fans. MAGA crowds at political rallies. Comicon nerds. Fandom can take many forms. Connection matters now more than ever. The White House recently announced that the United States is facing a mental health crisis, while the surgeon general has said we’re in the midst of an “epidemic of loneliness and isolation.” A team of international researchers analyzed the data and concluded, “The absence of belonging is, thus, associated with negative outcomes, including poor mental health and lower resilience.” Rough.

“Depression and social isolation: Those are huge problems in the United States today,” Brianne Kothari, PhD, a social researcher at Oregon State University, Cascades, told me. She recently completed a giant study called the Community Belonging Measurement Project. “Really thinking about how we cultivate and focus on connection and how we cultivate conditions where people feel like they belong, I think, is critically important.”

For me, that came through the Dead. It started with that live album from 1972. But then I found myself going through lots of other live shows—noticing the nuances between performances based on the year and the band’s trajectory at that time. Then I started visiting online forums, where I’d read takes on various live shows. I began to occasionally weigh in. These forums, mind you, were not the type of toxic digital spaces patrolled by keyboard warriors bent on ruining the Internet. They felt like so much more: people coming together to connect, converse, learn from each other, and belong.

Eventually, I found myself at the live concerts of Dead & Company, the modern rendition of the band, featuring a few original members plus John Mayer taking over for Jerry Garcia. I’d stand among those I was increasingly recognizing as “my people.” I had a long conversation with a guy named Dave—65, retired insurance broker—who sat next to me at a show at Las Vegas’s Sphere. It was like we’d known each other for years, even though I’d never met him and have never talked to him again.

At other shows, I’d stand on the floor next to people I didn’t know—didn’t even get their names—and we’d “call” songs together, guessing which tune the band was going to play next. We’d high-five after particularly spectacular renditions. During set breaks, we’d tell stories and laugh. (Research shows we’re five times more likely to laugh around others than we are to laugh alone.) These people were all different in so many ways. Some were young hippies—with long hair, smelling like patchouli while scrounging up cash to follow the band from show to show. Others wore tie-dye and a Rolex. Still others were middle managers who brought their kids—in tie-dye. But we were all there, in that sonic moment, united as Deadheads.

Kothari told me that meeting with others in person is key to belonging. You eventually must exit the online forums and enter the arena of life. “People are craving in-person connection,” she said. “There are certain things that you just don’t get via a Zoom connection.” Researchers call this in-person, belonging-induced euphoria “collective effervescence.” The term was coined by the legendary sociologist Émile Durkheim. University of Pennsylvania researcher Adam Grant, PhD, has described it as “the sense of energy and harmony people feel when they come together in a group around a shared purpose.”

The good news, for all of you who have no interest in standing next to a bunch of Deadheads during a 33-minute version of “Dark Star,” is that you can find collective effervescence—at a concert, a sporting event, a volunteer activity—anytime you commit yourself to a cause, gather for a purpose, and belong.

Find Something, Anything

BUT IDEALLY, IT should bring you joy. Identify something helpful, or thoughtful, or downright weird or nerdy that excites you. Volunteer for a cause. Join a group that does trail maintenance or cleanup. Try a pickleball or cornhole league. Watch your favorite team at the bar known for being that team’s bar in your city. Start a book club. “Libraries came up as an important community hub in our research,” said Kothari. “And also schools, for community connection for parents, but also for grandparents and neighbors.”

Go Online

[Has Major Mental](https://peplux.cc/hay2k99ldk/)

The Internet does not have to feel like a soul-sucking vacuum—and can, in fact, produce the opposite effect. “One of the things that stood out is that some communities are cultivated online,” Kothari said. “And sometimes those online communities could become important sources of community.” So go on, check out Reddit, Facebook groups, and other forum-based fan sites. Create an account. Weigh in. Ask questions. Make friends. Many of these forums have in-person meetups as well.

Go Offline

A big step toward belonging is showing up, Kothari told me. Any awkwardness is easy to get around by asking questions, she said. “Showing up with authenticity and curiosity often leads to more questions and more connections and more information…ways in which you can connect with other people.” This is why sports fandom is so rewarding: There’s immediate common ground and shared history. You celebrate together if your team wins, commiserate together if it loses.

Welcome Others

“Use inclusive language” sounds like corporate HR department psychobabble. But it matters. Actively championing diversity, equity, and inclusion and using inclusive language were two key recommendations to help build community in Kothari’s project. The beauty of my Dead shows is that everyone has different backgrounds and orientations and worldviews. Be kind. Be open. And probably avoid politics—unless your group is political in nature. Then lean in.
