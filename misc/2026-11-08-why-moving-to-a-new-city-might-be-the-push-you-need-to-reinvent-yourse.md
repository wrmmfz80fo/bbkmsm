# Why Moving to a New City Might Be the Push You Need to Reinvent Yourself, According To Men Who Did

FALL IS A SEASON OF CHANGE—and for some men, that shift goes beyond cooler weather. It can also mean stepping into a new chapter of selfhood, whether that’s coming out for the first time or finally letting go of an identity that no longer fits.

Maybe you're about to start college, ready to reinvent yourself—especially if you’ve not been able to live or embody your most authentic self back home. Or, perhaps you're moving to a new city and looking for a fresh start. Whatever stage of life or scenario you're in, exploring your sense of identity can be scary, daunting, and—as we recently saw in comedian Benito Skinner’s aptly-titled, self-inspired series Overcompensating—not always as straightforward as we might anticipate.

One thing is certain: Major life transitions can indeed spark identity shifts, explains Jeremy R. Goshorn, PhD, an assistant professor of psychology and clinical mental health counseling at Lebanon Valley College, who specializes working with LGBTQ+ folks. “Individuals tend to have the most identity development when they are challenged to rethink themselves or the way they operate,” he says.

“This often coincides with a large-scale change [such as] attending college, moving to a new city, moving out of a parental home, living with friends or a partner, a milestone birthday, the death of a loved one. [These] life events may serve as an impetus to evaluate and self-author oneself.”

And while that process can seem daunting, it can also be liberating. Here’s what it can look like at different stages of life—and how to support yourself (and others) through it.

College Can Be a Season of Reinvention

Starting college can feel like hitting reset. You’re away from home, surrounded by new people, and for many guys, it’s the first time you can fully explore who you are.

On paper, the research shows that 23 percent of Gen Z Americans self-identify as LGBTQ+, compared to recent research from The Harvard Crimson on the 2025 class at Harvard University, where it found that just under 30 percent of students surveyed identified as LGBTQ or openly ‘questioning’ their sexuality’. With Gen Z being the biggest demographic of people identifying as LGBTQ+ across the country, it’s clear that both those in and out of higher education are exploring sexuality with a sense of freedom previous generations didn’t have access to.

“College-aged people are in a profound moment of becoming,” says Taylor Orlandoni, adjunct professor of Applied Psychology at NYU Steinhardt School of Culture, Education and Human Development. “For LGBTQ+ students, the freedom of campus life intensifies the enormity of the shift from ‘home,’ to ‘campus.’ This is both a liberating and daunting engagement for queer or questioning first-year students.”

For Jared Fleming, his first year at university was a turning point, eventually empowering him to come out as gay at the start of his second year. But when he arrived at school, he had only ever dated girls: “I just wanted a pretty girlfriend so people would hopefully stop questioning my sexuality,” he tells Men's Health

With a lack of positive queer role models during his younger years, he felt trapped. Growing up, “gay meant weak, it meant wrong and I carried that fear and shame with me," says Fleming. "Coming out felt terrifying because once you do it you can’t take it back.”

For Fleming, college represented a time of much-needed self discovery that empowered him to share his identity with the world, on his own terms. “My whole personality had been built around not ‘seeming gay’…once I came out I could actually talk about people I was attracted to without lying,” he says. “Being myself became normal a lot faster than I ever expected and it can be easy to forget and take that freedom for granted now.”

How a New City Can Spark a New Chapter

Of course, not all reinventions happen at 18. For many, that moment comes later—and moving to a new city may be a helpful catalyst. For instance, many gay men relocate to cities like New York, Los Angeles, and San Francisco (which have the densest populations of LGBTQ+ folks), in order to find a queer community. And, even if it’s not one of these cities, simply moving to a new place can offer the opportunity for queer men to start fresh and reintroduce themselves to the world.

Although specific stats on how many queer men move to a new city in service of identity freedom aren't available, it’s clear that in the current political climate many LGBTQ+ are seeking relocation in the name of safety. In fact, some data shows that 38 percent of LGBTQ+ Americans considering relocating to a new state in search of safer conditions.

For Ben Jackson, filmmaker and co-creator of the short film Two Black Boys In Paradise, moving to a new place gave him the confidence to come out to his family at age 30. “Two years after I returned to the US from the UK I started dating and entertaining the idea of having an actual relationship with a guy,” he tells Men’s Health. “I didn’t yet know who that person would be, but I knew I wanted to be able to introduce them to my family.” At first, he felt guilty about coming out at a later age, because he felt he had lied to people close to him for a long time. “But I also felt such relief…I remember thinking I can finally be me.”

Reinvention certainly doesn’t have an age limit—29 percent of LGBTQ+ adults saying they came out after the age of 30—and many queer people embrace their identities for the first time well into their 40s or later.

Jaron Soh, Co-founder & CEO of LGBTQIA+ Mental Wellbeing App Voda has seen first hand how ‘reinvention’ when coming out later in life is best achieved by not rejecting one's past. “I’ve spoken with queer men who’ve come out later in life and in the process they grapple with their sense of self, or try to ‘reinvent’ a wholly new self. I find that ‘reinvention’ tends to be healthier when people try to integrate this new coming out chapter into the life they've already lived, and not by totally abandoning who they were.”

Despite the difficulties, all aspects of our personal histories can be used to become more authentic versions of ourselves in the future, says Soh. “The time you’ve spent hiding your sexuality could have been painful, but it is still a part of who you were and has led you to who you are today. That deserves honoring, too.”

But aside from the new adventures and possibilities open to men who come out later in life, there are still important factors to note such as having potentially lived with internalised feelings of shame for decades. “Be mindful of what comes up for you [when coming out] as many of us still carry internalized shame or outdated ideas about masculinity and relationships, so queer culture can be quite confronting for those of us who are just getting started,” says Soh. “Coming out can be a one-time process, but unlearning shame takes a lifetime.”

For some, however, coming out has allowed them to feel more jubilant about their identity. Mind Out, an LGBTQ+ Mental Health Charity interviewed over 200 LGBTQ+ people over 50, and found that some viewed aging itself a process for them to nurture their inner relationship with their identity, before sharing it outwardly. “I feel more comfortable in my skin and accepting of being LGBTQ+. I struggled with the concept when I was younger,” shared one participant.

The survey also found that for gay men over the age of 50, making sure there is space to socialize physically was a really important part of being able to live authentically in the later years of one's life. “I’m pleased to have a chance to go out and meet other gay men in my age group. It has helped a lot to combat loneliness,” one respondent to Mind Out’s research shared.

So, for the estimated 2.7 million LGBTQ+ people in the USA who are over the age of 50, reinvention is still very possible—it's not just reserved for those who are in their college years or early adulthood.

How to Show Up for Someone Reinventing Themselves

Whether at 18 or 58, one truth remains constant: Reinvention is rarely an entirely solo act. The support of friends, family, and community can make all the difference.

For loved ones there can be a moment of adaptation—especially if your child or friend is living a life that is unfamiliar to you hundreds of miles away. “You’ll want to be a neutral, grounded anchor for them. Your ability to be earnestly engaged with the very real uncertainty your friend or loved-one is experiencing is important,” says Orlandoni, noting that although there may be confusion or a desire to ask questions, this is a chance to meet them as their “authentic and vulnerable self” in ways that are ultimately exciting.

It’s also important to understand that coming out is for the person doing it more than the person receiving it, says Fleming. “If you find yourself thinking ‘why didn’t they tell me sooner?’ or ‘I didn’t want to know that,’ stop and ask why you’re so fixated on someone else’s sexuality. It quite literally doesn’t affect you. Sexuality is everything and nothing at the same time.”

Whether it’s at 18 or 58, reinvention isn’t about becoming someone new—it’s about finally living as yourself. That act of honesty isn’t just brave; it’s transformative.
