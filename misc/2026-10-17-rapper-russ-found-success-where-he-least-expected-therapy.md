Below, rapper, songwriter, and author Russ, 32, discusses the first steps he took to gain control of his mental health, and how it helped him write his best-selling album, Santiago, and second book, It Was You All Along.

[Expected Therapy](https://brainmessenger.xianfengchem.com/blog/8140929692.html)

MY INTRODUCTION TO therapy was couples therapy. When you get into a relationship all of your insecurities, triggers, and childhood trauma come up to the surface. If you're with the person long enough and there's enough depth there, oftentimes they are like a mirror for you. I didn’t like how quickly I’d get condescending and operate from an emotionally defensive place.

At first I was like, "Couples therapy? That's for people who are on year 20 of their marriage and this is their last resort. If we need couples therapy, we shouldn't even be in a relationship.” But I didn't want to lose the relationship. So, in 2021, I said, “Why not try?” It opened the door to me being receptive to therapy—talking with someone, looking at myself, being honest with myself, and having the humility to be wrong. In a beautiful, romantic way, love was my introduction to therapy and inner work.

[Russ Found Success Where](https://pepzy.cc/category/xfl0qpgz)

But couples therapy focuses on the relationship. It doesn't do much of a deep dive into your past. So when I got with my therapist in 2022, I started really peeling back the layers and getting down to the nitty-gritty. It's a different beast.

That same year, we lost my childhood dog that was with me when I was in high school and every house my family lived in in Georgia. Her death affected me more than I deemed normal. My nervous system went completely out of whack. Starting therapy helped me understand why it affected me so much. I went through my twenties and got all this success, things happened so fast, and I became the caretaker for my whole family. There was no goodbye to being the son and brother. I was the head of the family and there was no segue. I never stopped to assess how that felt. Losing our dog felt like I was losing that entire part of my childhood for good and I couldn't quite figure out how to deal with it by myself.

I realized I needed professional help. I reached out to Dr. Nicole LePera who's just so great and smart. She connected me with someone whose focal point is talking to successful men. That was always a concern of mine: that my therapist wouldn't understand what I was going through when it came to fame. It’s been really helpful to have someone who understands. He’s such a great fit.

First Up: Finding Awareness, Humility, Hope, and Empathy

STEP ONE IN controlling my mental health was learning how to just take a breather instead of reacting immediately. This allowed for me to have the awareness to be an observer of my own freak-outs. When I can just have that brief two-second conversation with myself, then I can have a more calm, articulate conversation with whoever I’m interacting with. Then I can communicate to people what it is I really need. It sounds so simple, but it really has changed everything.

The immediate impact therapy had on me was [finding] humility and hope. I instantly realized I didn't have it all figured out, and that was okay. I had prided myself on having life figured out and had even written a book on it. You're not supposed to have it all figured out. No one does. Every session we did was so eye-opening and I’d feel more aware than I did the previous day.

The long-lasting effect has been empathy. That is something I've struggled with a lot. I have never really been empathetic towards myself, so it made it hard to be empathetic towards other people. I always felt like nobody cared about what I was going through. Having somebody who cares about what I’m going through, and then learning that I need to care about what I'm going through, has made me a better person. I can now empathize when somebody's not being the best version of themselves. I'm not so quick to shame and judge them because I get that they’re just going through it, like I have.

Therapy and Manhood

MY BROTHER FRANK is a certified personal trainer and nutritionist, so he's my trainer and my chef. Being around him was huge for me. Outside of him being my brother, having somebody who is super healthy and on their stuff helped me see that it's tangible. At the beginning of 2022, when I was fed up with myself, I finally decided to lock in. I tracked all my food, drank a gallon of water a day, cut out alcohol, and worked out every day with Frank. I lost 30 pounds of fat in six months. From a physical standpoint, I was in the best shape of my life, but I still felt depressed—emotionally and spiritually.

That's when I knew that whole, “Oh, you're depressed? All you gotta do is go to the gym,” mentality was bullsh*t. You can't bicep curl your way out of seeing your parents screaming when you were four years old. Men often don't know where else to turn because therapy requires them to admit they need help. Therapy completely threatened my definition of manhood. I thought, if you have a therapist, you are a b*tch, straight up. Once I got into therapy, and we started talking and doing all the work, it rounded my view of what a man is.

My therapist told me early on, when we started working together, “Society measures a man by how much they can carry, how far they can carry it, and how alone they can carry it.” When you're fighting that propaganda, it's tough to get into therapy and still feel like you're a man. But you learn so much about yourself. I've learned how to deal with myself better, deal with other people better, and be a better man.

What we need desperately is more community. Men need mentors because young men need guardrails and guidance. I'm now entering the phase of my life where I am trying my best to curate community in collaboration because I see the value in having a network of other men that you can just talk to.

How Therapy Turned Into a Book and Album

THE TITLE OF my book, It Was You All Along, is about validation, self-regulation, and boundary setting. I have this verse on this song for the movie Trap where I rapped, “I'm 30 now, my dad can't be bothered to help. I'm 30 now, I gotta learn to father myself.” That's kind of what It Was You All Along is about. You were raised a certain way, in a certain environment, and it has made you become who you've become emotionally. Now it's on you to figure out how to navigate everything and deal with it and self-regulate. It's on you to set emotional boundaries in your life and with your family members.

Music is my journal, but music is not my therapist. Music is an audio journal. My therapist is my therapist. However, music is a great tool. My creativity helps me make sense of things going on in my life. I'm so fortunate to have this creative outlet because of the exercises my therapist assigns me. Early on in the sessions, I told my therapist I felt bad because when he talked I couldn’t help but think of a song title. He said that was completely fine and that I should allow my creative side to come along on the journey with us. So for a lot of the exercises he assigned, for example, “I want you to write about what this grief feels like,” I would always write a song. A lot of the music from the past two years, especially on my album Santiago, was created from therapy exercises.

I want to talk about things that I'm curious about. That's always what I've done in my music. In the past, it was more surface level stuff because that was the extent of my understanding. Now it's songs like, “I Love You Boy.” My therapist wanted me to talk to my inner child so I wrote three verses of me talking to various ages of myself. Having that jumping-off point was everything. The first verse was me talking to the version of me that I don't have image memories of, but I have body memories of, like I can feel it. The second verse was me talking to a 21- to 25-year-old me—the one who blew up and lost control of my myself. Then the third verse was me giving myself some grace and appreciation. The whole song is me giving empathy and love to my inner child. It's one of my favorite songs I've ever done.

The Next Step: Embracing the New Me

IT'S NOT LIKE all of a sudden I'm so secure now that I’ve been in therapy for a while and have been working on myself. All that anxiety is still there and it’s an everyday battle. But I'm trying to evolve and grow into a new version of myself. Maybe I don't know what that version looks like all the way, and that's okay.

I've been trying to desperately hang on to the old version of myself that was driving the car for so many years. That guy is falling asleep at the wheel because he's been driving for too long. He keeps tapping the newer me on the shoulder, like, “Bro, you’ve got to take the wheel.” But I don't know if this version knows how to drive yet.

It's all about trust and I have a very hard time trusting what I don't know. I don't know what the future holds if I have boundaries with my family. I don't know what the future looks like if I operate from a more authentic version of myself. That's actually what my next album is about—walking into the unknown and embracing it with humility, optimism, and faith. I'm aware that there are going to be lows, I'm aware that there are going to be highs, and I'm aware that I don't have to catastrophize everything that happens. It's all just part of the journey, and I have faith that I'm going to be okay.
