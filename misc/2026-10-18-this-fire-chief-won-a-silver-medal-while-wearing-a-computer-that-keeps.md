# This Fire Chief Won a Silver Medal—While Wearing a Computer That Keeps Him Alive

Edwin Smith was always an active guy. But when he went into sudden congestive heart failure, he couldn’t walk more than 10 feet without getting winded. Here, in his words, is how he regained his strength—and won second place in pickleball at the National Senior Games.

BEFORE I WENT into heart failure, I was as healthy as they came. I worked out regularly and played recreational sports in my free time. And, as the fire chief of Baton Rouge, I underwent a routine physical every year. I had 46 of those annual physicals in my lifetime—none of which ever found anything wrong.

That all changed, though, on the morning of August 17, 2018, when my body started shutting down. I climbed out of bed only to realize that my feet and ankles were so swollen that I couldn’t see my toes. I have a medical background as an EMT, so I knew I had to get to an emergency room right away.

It only took the doctor about 20 minutes to figure out I was having congestive heart failure. My liver and kidneys had all but shut down, and my heart was only working at about 10 percent of its normal output. I had plenty of questions, but my doctor asked me one first: “How did you make it here?”

As it turns out, I would be in and out of the hospital for the next four months. Right from the start, the doctors thought I was a candidate for heart transplant, but the process of getting a new heart is a long one. So in the meantime, the doctors decided to give me a left ventricular assist device (LVAD), a device that’s implanted in the heart in order to help it pump blood. (The device runs from your heart, through an opening in the skin, to an external computer, called a controller.) After the operation, the real work began. Here’s my advice for how to get to it:

Start Slow (Really, It’s Okay)

In the fall of 2018, I could barely walk 10 feet without being out of breath—so I had no choice but to start off slow. I started walking every day. Outside. In Louisiana. And let me tell you, walking in 98-degree heat isn’t fun. I also had to carry a secondary pack of equipment (batteries, clips, an extra computer) with me whenever I went in case my LVAD controller stopped working. But: I did it.

Do Something You Love

After I had regained some strength, my wife and I joined a local health club, where I ran into a few men I’d worked with at the fire station. They told me that there were regular pickleball games every Monday and Friday, and encouraged me to play with them. At first, I wanted nothing to do with it. I was still dealing with some shortness of breath, and I didn’t know how much I could exert myself. But when my wife started playing, I eventually gave in, and started volleying back and forth with a friend. That’s when I discovered that I really loved pickleball.

Make It Competitive

Pickleball was good for me. It gave me something to be competitive about, and I hadn’t had that in a while. I played everyone at the club. In the beginning, they were all so patient with me. Eventually, I got stronger and my stamina picked up. I also got used to carrying around my LVAD equipment on the court. What’s my secret? Compression shorts with pockets. I put my batteries where the thigh pads go, and my computer where the hip pads go. Total game changer, no pun intended.

I do know my limits, though. I don’t play singles. Only doubles. There’s something about the camaraderie of having a partner out there with you—plus, there’s less court to cover. Eventually, my partner and I got so confident in our skills that we tried out for the National Senior Games, and qualified.

Don’t Set a Limit for Yourself

My pickleball partner and I competed at the National Senior Games this summer, in Des Moines, Iowa. There were more than 2,000 pickleball athletes there. At first, we didn’t think we’d get very far—we lost our first match, so we were playing in the loser’s bracket right away. But then we won seven matches in a row, and ended up coming in second place—taking home the silver medal.

Focus on the Future

I focused a lot on my physical activity, but I also made changes to my diet. You have to, when you have heart failure. Now, I’m basically on a low- to no-salt diet. That’s tough to do anywhere, but it’s especially tough to do in Louisiana. I hardly ever eat fried food anymore. I grill my food or bake it. And after a while, my taste buds adjusted. Sure, it’s tough when you’re at a crawfish bake with your whole family and you’re just sitting there, staring at them. But then I look over at my grandkids, and I have all the motivation I need to keep going. To keep living.

I’m still on the list for a heart transplant. I plan to get one, too, even though the LVAD is doing its job, and more. I still don’t know why my heart failed; even now, the doctors can’t give me a reason. It’s a hard thing to wrap your head around, but I’m moving on. I’m thankful for every day I have, and I’m looking forward to having a lot more of them.
