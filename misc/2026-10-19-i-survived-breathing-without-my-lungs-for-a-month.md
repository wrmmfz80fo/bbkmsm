AJAY RANDHAWA, A 39-year-old from Sacramento, California, worked as a respiratory therapist for almost a decade. He nearly made it through 2020 without catching COVID-19 from the infected patients at his hospital.

“I was the last one in my department to even get sick,” he says.

When he tested positive that December, he was just four days shy of receiving his first COVID shot. He was disappointed, but not alarmed, at least at first. He was healthy and had helped others kick COVID. But he started going downhill fast, with excruciating headaches and vomiting. He went to the Emergency Department at UC Davis Health and got an X-ray of his lungs.

“Everybody in the room went silent,” he says. “The X-ray popped up on the screen, and it was worse than any X-ray I had seen or prepped for when taking care of COVID patients.”

The image, and later CT scans, showed lungs that looked fuzzy and opaque instead of clear, dark, and hollow. With his lungs severely compromised, intubation was the next step. But Randhawa refused based on what he had seen happen to patients with lungs as bad as his.

Instead, for two weeks, he lay on his stomach in a hospital bed with oxygen shooting up his nose at a high flow rate. He was panting at around 50 to 60 breaths per minute. He was covered in ice packs to cool him off as he maintained a fever that hovered between 102 and 103 degrees Fahrenheit. His lungs kept getting worse because of a cytokine storm, a flooding of excessive inflammatory immune system molecules that damage tissue.

He would test positive for COVID for roughly 20 days. After nearly 17 days trying to avoid intubation, he couldn’t breathe on his own anymore. He’d lost 60 pounds from breathing so fast he was spent.

On January 3, 2021, with failing lungs, he called his family. He told them the worst might be yet to come, and he wouldn’t be able to talk to them for a while. But he promised he would be home by Valentine’s Day, an improbable goal based on how long he had already been in the intensive care unit.

The next time he woke up, the board on his wall said February 3. He was only alive because he’d spent weeks on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO).

ECMO can be used to buy time when someone’s lungs are failing so fast that the pressure of a standard ventilator could damage them, says Jason Yeates Adams, MD, a professor of medicine at UC Davis.

“ECMO essentially creates a lung outside the body that can sustain the patient while waiting for other treatments and for the body’s natural recovery processes to heal the lungs,” says Dr. Adams.

To start ECMO, doctors place a flexible tube into a major blood vessel to pull three to five liters of blood out of the body per minute. The blood runs through a machine that adds oxygen gas, removes carbon dioxide, and then ejects the blood using a centrifugal pump. A second tube enters another vein to supply the heart.

“It sort of sprays that blood back into the right side of the heart, where it then travels through the lungs that are completely deflated and dysfunctional,” says Dr. Adams.

Blood vessels in the lungs, often the only parts still working, then carry the freshened blood to the left side of the heart to pump oxygen back out to the body.

First used in the 1970s, ECMO became available in select hospitals for patients with lung problems from influenza, pneumonia, and heart failure. For years, many hospitals had ECMO machines that went unused for days or weeks at a time, says Dr. Adams. COVID changed that.

“In COVID, so many people were succumbing to respiratory failure that it was no longer an intermittent therapy,” says Dr. Adams. “We always had people on ECMO.”

Administering ECMO isn’t easy. It always requires at least two nurses. One slightly misplaced tube can be fatal. Blood clots can happen often.

“We just got more sophisticated at being able to get all the pieces needed to do ECMO well to occur consistently and in sync with one another because we were practicing so much more,” says Dr. Adams.

When Randhawa learned he’d spent weeks on ECMO, he was shocked.

“Before that, in my field, I knew ECMO as a last-ditch effort,” says Randhawa. “Usually, prior to COVID, if you were using it for an emergency case, it was like a dignity to the family to buy some time to say goodbye.”

Randhawa suffered and recovered from multiple blood clots. In 23 days on ECMO, he’d spent 20 showing minimal improvement. His stay on ECMO was so complicated that his family prepared to say goodbye. When a blood clot in the ECMO circuit was headed toward his head, doctors warned his family that he needed to improve that night so they could take him off ECMO, or he would die.

“That night, my body decided to turn around completely, did a 180 for the first time, and I was somehow showing better numbers everywhere,” he says. The doctors disconnected him from the machine

When he woke up, he had a tube coming out of his throat. His muscles had withered from his 6’4” frame. He weighed just 160 pounds instead of his normal 220. He couldn’t move or talk and had to be fed with a spoon. He was told he would need two months of rehab before going home, but that wasn’t fast enough to fulfill his promise to his family.

With the encouragement and support of medical staff and family, he pushed through physical therapy sessions. Every time physical therapists assigned him exercises, he asked for more. He hit his physical improvement goals early and was allowed to go home on February 13, one day earlier than he’d promised. Though he could barely walk up the stairs, he shocked his family by returning home before Valentine’s Day.

Randhawa has been told that he was one of the sickest people to go on ECMO, but one of the luckiest to recover. Now, he has focused on lifting weights, eating whole foods, and learning about supplements such as creatine that have helped him rebuild his body. He’s keeping another promise he made to his wife.

“I said that if I make it out of this, I’m going to get into the greatest shape of my life,” he says.

Randhawa is working with a therapist for post-traumatic stress disorder and hopes to begin training others to better their bodies and health. For now, his primary focus is on his health and his family. He lifts weights multiple times a week—he says it saved his life—and he enjoys every moment he can with his daughter, Asa, and son, Arz.

Since the pandemic has waned, doctors are using what they learned during COVID to use ECMO in new, lifesaving ways. For example, ECMO can be used in some patients with cardiac arrest. It can be a bridge to a heart transplant or an artificial heart implant.

“ECMO has gone from being relatively inaccessible to something that, at most major health systems, is now a standard tool in the toolkit used to help people survive the most catastrophic illnesses,” says Dr. Adams.
