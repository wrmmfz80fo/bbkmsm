# The Workout That Brought a Wounded Warrior Back to Life

This story is part of our ongoing “First Steps” series, where we share extraordinary stories of men who transformed their bodies, minds, and lives with a focus on the first steps it took them to get there (because, after all, nothing can change without a first step!). Read all of the stories here.

When Dan Nevins, now 50, was deployed in Iraq as a squad leader, an IED detonated under his vehicle, resulting in a traumatic brain injury and ultimately an amputation of both his legs below the knee. For about 10 years, he says he “found ways to thrive by self-medicating with accomplishments and successes.” Things like climbing Kilimanjaro and riding century rides (100 milers) on an un-adapted road bike.

At the time, he’d have said things were going OK. But in 2013, when he needed complete revision surgery on his right residual limb, the activity/accomplishment cycle ground to a halt and he landed “in a very dark place.” Nevins explains how what happened next—and how it transformed his mindset and life’s mission—shocked even him.

WHEN I WAS "grounded" after that complete revision surgery, and I had to rehab for 8 weeks while home alone. I was divorced, my oldest daughter had just joined the Army herself, and I couldn’t physically take care of my 3-year-old daughter while hopping around on one prosthetic leg and crutches. And by needing to take FMLA leave for the surgery, I wasn’t allowed to email or call my team. It was the silence and aloneness that made me start to have nightmares, and the lack of ability to self-medicate with adventure led me into a very dark place. I started taking Benadryl and chasing it down with whiskey just to get to sleep. Apparently, things I had built a wall around started leaking through.

I HAD RANDOMLY called a friend to tell someone what I was feeling. When she said, “You need some yoga in your life,” I nearly lost it. I didn’t take that recommendation well, but instead of giving up, she suggested meditation, which was much more palatable for me than yoga. I had read in publications like the Harvard Business Review that meditation could help your brain. I was so out of sorts that I was kind of desperate to try anything that might work. And I knew it couldn’t hurt.

By the third day of meditating, the nightmares stopped, and my overall outlook started to improve. After the 8-week recovery, when I was able to put my prosthetic back on and go back to work, that is when I called her to say “thank you” for giving me that tool, that’s when she said, “I think you owe me some yoga.” I couldn’t think of a way to get out of it, so I committed to 3 private lessons.

My first yoga class was torture. I hated it. I was still sore from surgery and wearing prosthetics and I wasn’t doing well at it, which made it worse, because I don’t like being bad at anything.

I went back, though, because of my commitment to take three private lessons. When I went to that second class, I was more committed than ever that it wasn’t my thing. I had had a terrible experience (mostly because of my own resistance), but it was also painful…and hot…and I sucked at it… and she was saying weird yoga stuff, like “root down, to rise up” which just sounded like nonsense at the time.

At first, in that second class, it sucked just as hard. I got so frustrated that I asked if I could try it with my prosthetic legs off. In taking off my legs and finally feeling stable and grounded, that got me to have one of the most profound and visceral, legitimate life-changing experiences I’ve ever had. By finally giving in and submitting to the practice—and allowing myself to actually “do the thing” instead of resisting—it was like the whole universe exploded inside of me and lit me up from the inside out. I went from feeling the smallest and most vulnerable I’ve ever felt (on my knees with what felt like the hideous, what-was-left-of-my-legs out) with this 5’4 yoga teacher towering over me, to the most powerful and alive I’ve ever felt, all with one yoga pose (Warrior One), and a major attitude shift.

What I Never Expected From Yoga

AFTER THAT I was hooked and signed up for teacher training a week later. Yoga, especially teacher trainings, has left me with an abundance of patience and the ability to stay grounded and centered in situations that would have set me off before.

Before I took yoga, I was doing good things in my life, but a lot of it didn’t mean much…it’s like everything was flat. Once I did these teacher trainings, it’s almost like I went from black and white to 3-D, fully-animated color.

Prior to learning yoga, life was sort of “fast and furious” I was always on the road for work—or creating work for myself as an excuse to do more “things” or constantly in search of a party or an excuse to drink and have fun. There was a lot of fun chasing, which isn’t necessarily “bad,” but it definitely wasn’t healthy.

The physical practice of yoga—connecting breath to movement—has a profound effect on the regulation of my nervous system, and the positive result of strenuous physical exercise is great for my body (healthy body, healthy mind). As a result of experience, it the physical/tangible benefits of the practice, it basically allows every aspect of me to function better. Better quality of thoughts, better habits, better decision making, better partner, better father. The benefits manifest in many ways. Even on the road. I used to experience road rage on almost every drive. Now, it’s basically non-existent.

The Record Scratch

ALL OF THIS got a major test from November 2021 until the beginning of 2023. I was diagnosed with Stage III colon cancer, apparently from toxic exposure from combat. I had massive complications that led to multiple surgeries that completely wrecked my core, not to mention almost dying from it. My recovery and rehab took more than a year, and I am just recently able to get back to my physical practice without serious worry of injuring myself. At one point, I realized I’d been forgetting to meditate. When I brought back the practice, meditation kept me sane during my recovery.

What I Tell Guys Who Are Where I Used To Be

WHEN YOU HAVE something so significant happen to you in the field, like getting your legs blown off or having a brain injury, it’s almost like an imprint of “this is who you are.” You’re the amputee; the combat-wounded guy. People look at you a certain way and expect you to be a certain way, and you can sort of fall into it. For me, it wasn’t necessarily uncomfortable. But that experience on my yoga mat, and then going to yoga teacher training, helped me realize that I can be anything I want. I can choose another whole reality for myself. I’ll always be a guy that was wounded in combat and has prosthetic legs. But that doesn’t mean I have to behave in a way that people would expect of someone with those experiences.

I now share my experiences as a professional keynote speaker and serve as an advocate, supporter and ambassador for the Wounded Warrior Project, and I'm a yoga teacher as well. Everyone is living with invisible wounds, and my goal is to help fellow warriors and all people who are suffering realize they, too, can overcome their struggles and find a new purpose in life.

When I meet people and warriors who are in a tough place and want to get out of it, my advice is to not think, just do. Take action. Do the thing, whatever it is. Analysis paralysis is a real thing, and it thwarts and constrains our greatness in all areas. I used to do it all the time and that way of being has held me back in too many situations to count. “Not doing” is giving in to fear, and operating from fear is the most life limiting way of being. I still catch myself doing the same. But breaking through fear is the most powerful thing we can do as humans and the rewards are always worth the effort.
